> **A note on timing:** This post is adapted from a reflection written near the close of the PhD admissions cycle for **Fall 2025 entry ("25 Fall")**. It preserves my perspective at nineteen, when Rice was still a possible next step. References to the present belong to that period; the page date records the preparation of this English version.

This is my first time writing something quite like this here. I thought I would write a summary of my application season, but I keep finding myself drawn to things that have little to do with admissions decisions. So perhaps this will simply be a conversation with myself.

For me, the Fall 2025 admissions cycle is almost over. There is still time left to wait, but I do not think it will change the choice I have already begun to make. My next beginning may be at Rice. Before that, I want to look back for a moment at how I arrived here.

I grew up with very little. Before I was born, my family moved to a county town to get away from certain circumstances. That was where I spent my childhood. Between my age and our financial situation, I barely attended kindergarten. Even staying in primary school was something we had to fight for: I took tests to prove that I could keep up with the older children, and my mother pleaded for me to be allowed to remain.

I have few bright memories of primary school. Outside my studies, much of what I remember is bullying and a Chinese-language teacher whose treatment of children depended on their family backgrounds. I did not know much about how other children lived. If you have rarely seen a world in color, it can be difficult to explain how gray your own has been.

During those years, I became seriously ill and needed an operation my family could not afford. I remember lying in a hospital bed and watching my mother kneel before a doctor, begging for help. Many details of my childhood have faded. That image has stayed.

Adults often praised me for being mature for my age. I had little chance to discover what it might be like to grow up without having to be.

In middle school, the pressure became so overwhelming that I attempted suicide. I was found and survived. What frightened me most was the possibility that I might no longer be able to continue my education. At the time, I felt I had almost nothing. Studying was the one source of hope in a world that seemed very small, and I was terrified of losing it.

Gradually, I worked my way from near the bottom of my year to the top few dozen students and earned a place at a selective high school. My progress was easy enough to describe in rankings. What I could not put into words was harder to find on a report card.

For almost all three years of high school, I ranked first in my class. To other people, I must have seemed dependable, promising, easy to be proud of—the child parents held up as an example. What I remember most, though, is numbness and pain. In that rigid, exam-driven environment, I became very good at meeting expectations and increasingly unsure of who I was.

I knew how to be a good student. I did not really know how to be a child.

I wanted to feel like a whole person. Almost without noticing, I began to imitate the people around me: how they expressed their feelings, how they spoke to one another, how they seemed to know what to do. I was trying to learn something about being human that I felt I had missed.

Eventually, I entered one of China's 985 universities and chose chemistry, a major I had often heard people warn against. University gave me a little more room to breathe. I maintained a rank of around third in my year, and, after a number of experiences along the way, I decided to prepare for a PhD.

I began in polymer materials and later moved into theoretical chemistry during an exchange at Berkeley. Along the way, I met many kind and interesting teachers and older students. When I think of that period, they give me warm memories to return to.

Changing fields takes only a few words to describe. Living through it meant starting over in many ways: unfamiliar ideas, different approaches to research, and the pressure of applications drawing closer. Financial uncertainty was always there as well.

I saved much of the money for the exchange a little at a time, working temporary jobs. While I was still studying in China, I had already stopped relying on my family for living expenses. Studying, doing research, and finding a way to support myself were never neatly separate parts of life. They had to be worked out together, one thing at a time.

There are many stories inside those years. Some would take too long to tell here. Others are difficult to explain even when I find the words for what happened. Somehow, the application season has reached this point, and I can finally pause long enough to think about where I might go next.

I do not know what that next life will look like. The possibility of Rice brings both excitement and unease. I suspect it will take time to understand the habits and feelings I have carried with me. I still want to study things that interest me. I also want to find out who I can be beyond grades, rankings, and application results.

I will leave the story here for now. Let this be a small record of a particular moment: the end of the Fall 2025 admissions cycle, and a nineteen-year-old looking back at the road already traveled while trying to imagine the life still to come.

Just a lonely nineteen-year-old student, with a little more left to say. Meow.
